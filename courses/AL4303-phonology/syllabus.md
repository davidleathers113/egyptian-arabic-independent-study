# Syllabus — AL4303 Principles of Phonological Analysis — Egyptian Arabic

## Course purpose

Develop practical skill in discovering, testing, and defending the sound system of a language from data rather than from spelling, intuition, AI output, or preexisting grammatical claims. The course is modeled on DIU's published AL4303 competency boundary while adding a deliberately documented independent-study structure.

## DIU-aligned scope

The course must provide repeated practical work in:

1. phonetic (etic) vs. phonological (emic) evidence;
2. distinctive features and natural classes;
3. phonetic plausibility;
4. complementary distribution;
5. free variation;
6. contrast in identical and analogous environments;
7. phonological processes and conditioning environments;
8. typological comparison/universals;
9. tone analysis;
10. morphophonemics;
11. implications of phonological analysis for orthography decisions.

The independent study intentionally stops short of turning into DIU's later advanced phonology course. Optimality Theory, feature geometry, autosegmental representations, metrical phonology, and related frameworks are introduced only to the extent that they sharpen analysis or prepare the learner to recognize later graduate-level work.

## Workload

- 135 documented hours
- 12 instructional weeks
- target average: 11.25 hours/week
- at least 60% of time should involve active analysis, problem sets, elicitation, hypothesis testing, correction, or assessment
- reading is preparation for analysis, not a substitute for analysis

## Required working library

### Primary field-analysis text

**Donald A. Burquest, _Phonological Analysis_, 3rd ed., SIL International.** Prefer the current 2025 SIL release/eBook when available. The course uses Burquest systematically because its chapter sequence moves from phonemic analysis into morphophonemics, natural processes, syllable conditioning, larger domains, autosegmental phonology, and metrical phonology, with problems in most major chapters.

### Modern secondary text

**Carlos Gussenhoven & Haike Jacobs, _Understanding Phonology_, 5th ed. (Routledge, 2024).** Use selectively for modern terminology, typology, underlying forms, distinctive features, syllables/moras, tone, stress, and prosodic structure. Its exercises provide additional practice, but Burquest remains the primary procedural backbone.

### Tone practicum text

**Keith L. Snider, _Tone Analysis for Field Linguists_.** Use selected introductory, methodology, and pitch-analysis chapters for the dedicated tone practicum. If a verified second edition is available, use it; otherwise the 2018 SIL edition is sufficient for the assigned material.

### Free review/reference

**_Essentials of Linguistics_, 2nd ed., Chapter 4: Phonology.** Use as an open-access review and self-check resource for phonemes/allophones, phonotactics, contrast, complementary distribution, phonemic analysis, rules, derivations, and common rule types. It is not the depth standard for the course.

### Stretch problem source

**MIT OpenCourseWare 24.961 — Introduction to Phonology (Michael Kenstowicz, Fall 2014).** Use selected lecture notes and problem sets only after the corresponding core skill is learned. This is a graduate-level course and therefore functions as stretch material, not the baseline expectation.

## Core domains

1. Phonetics vs. phonology; etic vs. emic evidence.
2. Contrast in identical and analogous environments.
3. Complementary distribution and free variation.
4. Phonemes, allophones, and competing analyses.
5. Distinctive features and natural classes.
6. Phonological rules/processes and conditioning environments.
7. Phonotactics, syllable structure, and neutralization.
8. Stress, prosodic domains, and larger-unit conditioning.
9. Tone analysis with genuine non-Arabic tonal data.
10. Morphophonemics and underlying representations.
11. Orthographic implications and limits of phonological evidence.
12. Responsible uncertainty and evidential argumentation.

## Cross-linguistic breadth requirement

Egyptian Arabic is the longitudinal field language, but **at least half of graded formal problem-set work must use non-Arabic languages**. The final portfolio must include an index showing the language, phenomenon, and analytical skill practiced for each cross-linguistic dataset.

A dedicated tone practicum must use a true tone language. Egyptian Arabic intonation/stress work cannot substitute for this requirement.

## Egyptian Arabic role

Egyptian Arabic supplies longitudinal data from the consultant/corpus used in AL4302. The learner must continually distinguish:

- observed phone/form;
- proposed phoneme or representation;
- distribution/environment;
- candidate process;
- lexical evidence;
- morphological evidence;
- counterexamples;
- competing analyses;
- confidence level;
- next elicitation needed.

### Analysis-first embargo

For each major Egyptian Arabic phenomenon:

1. inspect and organize the primary data;
2. write a provisional analysis;
3. freeze/version that analysis;
4. only then consult published descriptions;
5. document agreement, disagreement, and revision rationale.

Published Egyptian Arabic scholarship is therefore sequenced deliberately in the reading plan rather than front-loaded as an answer key.

## Orthography module

The Week 11 orthography memo is intentionally limited. A sound phonological analysis does **not** by itself determine a good orthography. The module therefore includes SIL work on phonological depth and non-linguistic factors such as stakeholder acceptance and usability. The assignment is a decision memo identifying options, evidence, tradeoffs, and missing community input—not an attempt to redesign Arabic orthography.

## Assessment model

- cross-linguistic problem sets: 25%
- Egyptian Arabic analysis notebook/labs: 15%
- midterm practical: 10%
- tone practicum: 10%
- morphophonemic case study: 10%
- orthography decision memo: 5%
- AI benchmark: 5%
- final phonological sketch: 15%
- final practical/oral defense: 5%

## Passing/mastery standard

85% overall **and** demonstrated competence in:

- contrast/distribution analysis;
- feature/natural-class reasoning;
- process formulation and evidence;
- syllable/phonotactic analysis;
- introductory tone analysis;
- morphophonemics.

A high average cannot compensate for a major unmastered core domain. Remediate and retest weak domains.
