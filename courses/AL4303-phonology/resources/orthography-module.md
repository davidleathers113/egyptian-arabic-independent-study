# Week 11 Orthography Decision Module

## Why this module exists

DIU describes AL4303 as particularly relevant for field workers preparing to develop or revise an orthography. That does **not** mean a phonological analysis mechanically outputs a writing system.

This module teaches a narrower and more defensible skill:

> Given a phonological analysis, identify an orthographic decision, explain the linguistic options, identify non-linguistic constraints, and state what stakeholder/community evidence is still required.

The learner is not designing a replacement orthography for Egyptian Arabic.

# Required reading

## 1. Michael Cahill — “Non-linguistic factors in orthographies” (2014)

SIL record:
https://togo-benin.sil.org/resources/archives/56610

Core lesson:
An effective orthography must be not only linguistically sound but acceptable to stakeholders and usable in practice. Political, sociolinguistic, educational, psycholinguistic, and technological factors matter.

## 2. Keith L. Snider — “Orthography and phonological depth” (2014)

SIL record:
https://togo-benin.sil.org/resources/archives/56613

Core lesson:
The decision is not always a simple choice between a maximally shallow phonemic representation and a deep morphophonemic representation. The level of representation chosen has practical consequences.

## 3. Cahill & Rice, eds. — _Developing Orthographies for Unwritten Languages_ (2014)

SIL book/TOC record:
https://togo-benin.sil.org/resources/archives/56046

Read the introduction if accessible and use the volume as a map of the wider orthography problem.

# Assignment: Orthography Decision Memo

**Length:** 1,500–2,500 words plus tables/examples.

Choose **one limited issue** from a documented language dataset. A non-Arabic field-language case is preferred because Egyptian Arabic already has established orthographic traditions and this exercise is about learning the reasoning process, not proposing reform.

Possible issues:

- whether an allophonic distinction should be represented;
- representation of a contrast with difficult/rare characters;
- whether predictable vowel alternations should be written consistently or phonetically;
- tone marking strategy in a small tonal dataset;
- treatment of morphophonemic alternation;
- word-boundary decision;
- treatment of loan phonemes;
- competing representations for a contrast.

---

# Required memo structure

## 1. Decision statement

State the exact decision to be made in one sentence.

Example form:

> Should contrast X be represented by separate graphemes in the proposed practical orthography?

## 2. Linguistic evidence

Include:

- contrast/distribution;
- functional load where evidence permits;
- alternations;
- phonological depth considerations;
- morphophonemic consequences;
- ambiguity introduced or removed;
- predictable vs. unpredictable information.

Do not overstate what the dataset proves.

## 3. Candidate options

Give at least two viable options.

For each option state:

- what is represented;
- what is left predictable/unwritten;
- advantages;
- disadvantages;
- reading/writing burden;
- technical/font/input issues where relevant.

## 4. Non-linguistic evidence needed

Identify what cannot be answered from phonology alone:

- community preference;
- existing literacy traditions;
- relationship to regional/national scripts;
- education policy;
- religious/literary traditions;
- keyboard/font/device availability;
- acceptability to writers/readers;
- ease of teaching;
- dialect/sociolect politics.

## 5. Provisional recommendation

Give a provisional recommendation **conditioned on missing evidence**.

Preferred style:

> On the linguistic evidence alone, Option B has advantages X and Y. However, no final recommendation should be made until native-speaker literacy testing and stakeholder consultation address Z.

## 6. Validation plan

Describe how the decision would be tested:

- reading task;
- writing task;
- comprehension/ambiguity task;
- preference interviews;
- teaching trial;
- iteration with community members.

# Grading rubric

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Correct phonological analysis | 25% |
| Quality of competing options | 15% |
| Phonological-depth reasoning | 15% |
| Recognition of non-linguistic factors | 20% |
| Evidence/uncertainty discipline | 15% |
| Validation plan and clarity | 10% |

A memo that proposes a technically elegant spelling but ignores community acceptability/usability cannot receive an A.
