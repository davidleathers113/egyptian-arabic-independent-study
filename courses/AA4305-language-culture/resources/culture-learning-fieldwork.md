# Culture-Learning Fieldwork Method

## Purpose

Turn `culture acquisition` into a disciplined practice of participation, observation, interpretation, host-perspective seeking, and revision—without reducing Egyptian life to stereotypes or treating a language partner as a representative of everyone.

The method is adapted from participant-observation/ethnographic disciplines, especially James P. Spradley's Developmental Research Sequence, for **learner development rather than formal ethnographic research**.

---

# The core distinction

Always separate:

## Observation
What was directly seen/heard?

> At 6:10 p.m., SPK001 declined the first offer of X and accepted after a second offer.

## Interpretation
What might it mean?

> I wondered whether repeated offering is expected politeness.

## Host perspective
What did a host person say about it?

> SPK001 explained that in this situation...

## Generalization confidence
How broadly can the claim responsibly be extended?

> Low: one interaction/one speaker. Need more contexts/speakers.

This four-part separation is mandatory in graded culture notes.

---

# Culture hypothesis ledger

Maintain a table:

| ID | Domain | Observation | Interpretation | Host perspective(s) | Counterexample | Confidence | Next evidence needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Confidence:
- `very low` — first observation/idea;
- `low` — repeated but narrow context;
- `moderate` — multiple instances/perspectives with known limits;
- `high` — substantial converging evidence, still context-bounded.

Avoid `certain` for broad cultural claims.

---

# Domains worth observing

Only pursue domains that arise naturally or matter to actual participation.

Possible domains:

- greetings/farewells;
- forms of address;
- hospitality/food/drink;
- invitations/refusals;
- giving/receiving help;
- requests/favors;
- thanks/apologies;
- compliments;
- disagreement/correction;
- humor/teasing;
- family references;
- age/status/respect;
- privacy/personal questions;
- time/planning;
- money/payment/service encounters;
- friendship/reciprocity;
- religious expressions/social language;
- workplace interaction;
- online/voice-message communication.

This is not a checklist to complete. It is a menu for real questions.

# Weekly fieldnote format

## 1. Situation
- date/time;
- setting;
- participants represented by safe IDs;
- relevant relationship/context;
- whether the event was structured lab or normal participation.

## 2. Thick-enough concrete description
Write what happened in sequence before interpreting motives.

Include exact short language examples only when privacy/consent permits.

## 3. Learner reaction
What surprised, confused, pleased, embarrassed, or frustrated you?

This matters because your own expectations are part of the encounter.

## 4. Interpretation hypotheses
List **multiple possible interpretations** where reasonable.

## 5. Host perspective
If appropriate, ask open-endedly:
- “What was happening there?”
- “Would different people do this differently?”
- “When would someone not say/do that?”
- “Does age/relationship/setting matter?”

Avoid leading questions designed to confirm your theory.

## 6. Contrast/counterexample
Look for:
- same action in different relationship;
- same function with different wording;
- another speaker's perspective;
- urban/regional/generational difference;
- situation where expected pattern does not occur.

## 7. Revised hypothesis
State the narrowest claim supported by current evidence.

## 8. Language implications
What vocabulary, pragmatics, formulaic language, register, or interactional skill would improve participation in this domain?

# Perspective diversity

When a culture-heavy conclusion matters, seek more than one source where feasible:

- coach/partner;
- another Egyptian speaker;
- naturally observed interaction;
- reputable Egyptian-authored media/resource;
- scholarly description when relevant.

Do not recruit extra people merely to satisfy a numerical quota. The goal is to **discover variation**, not manufacture consensus.

---

# Identity and scope labels

When context matters, use precise descriptions such as:

- `this speaker`;
- `this family`;
- `this friend group`;
- `this Cairo workplace context`;
- `this age group in the observed setting`;

rather than immediately writing:

- `Egyptians...`;
- `Arabs...`;
- `Muslims...`;
- `Christians...`.

Broader claims require correspondingly broader evidence.

---

# Cultural misunderstanding postmortem

When something goes wrong, write:

1. What did I intend?
2. What did I say/do?
3. What response occurred?
4. What did I initially think happened?
5. What did the other person say happened, if discussed?
6. What linguistic factors might matter?
7. What cultural/pragmatic factors might matter?
8. What relationship/personal factors might matter?
9. What will I try differently next time?
10. What would count as evidence that my new interpretation is better?

Do not automatically classify every uncomfortable interaction as `culture shock` or every misunderstanding as a cultural rule.

---

# Culture-learning case-study rubric

A strong case study:

- provides concrete observations;
- distinguishes fact from interpretation;
- includes host perspective;
- considers variation/counterevidence;
- avoids moralizing/essentializing;
- connects culture and language pragmatically;
- documents revision of the learner's own assumptions;
- protects privacy;
- ends with a more effective participation strategy.

A weak case study is a list of facts copied from the internet.

---

# Ethics

The language partner is a person first, not research material.

Do not:
- publish private stories without explicit permission;
- pressure someone to discuss religion/politics/family/private matters;
- turn casual friendship into undisclosed public research;
- publish identifying fieldnotes;
- use AI on private stories/transcripts without appropriate permission;
- imply one person's perspective represents their entire culture.

The best evidence of culture acquisition is often **better relationship and more appropriate participation**, not a more elaborate taxonomy.
