# Project — AI as an Egyptian Arabic Language Coach

## Research question

Where can current AI systems productively support **learner-directed Egyptian Arabic acquisition**, and where do they diverge from native-speaker language, cultural judgment, or relational participation?

Secondary question:

> Does AI use increase the learner's ability/willingness to participate with real people, or does it mainly create a comfortable parallel practice environment?

## Research-design rule

**Freeze the protocol before reading the Week 11 AI/CALL outcome literature.**

Record:
- tools/models/versions where visible;
- test date;
- exact task definitions;
- prompts/settings that materially affect results;
- evaluation rubric;
- which items will receive native-speaker review.

This reduces the temptation to design an experiment that reproduces findings already read.

---

# Test domains

Choose 4–6.

## 1. Role-play / interaction practice
Test whether AI can sustain an Egyptian-Arabic-only or Egyptian-dominant interaction at appropriate difficulty.

Measure:
- dialect consistency;
- responsiveness;
- interactional usefulness;
- repair behavior;
- whether learner uses language later with a person.

## 2. Correction of learner-produced Egyptian Arabic
Give the same samples to AI and native speaker where appropriate.

Track:
- real error detected;
- false error invented;
- valid alternative incorrectly rejected;
- Egyptian form “corrected” toward MSA;
- correction technically possible but unnatural;
- explanation quality;
- correction overload.

## 3. Naturalness / register judgments
Use controlled alternatives.

Ask:
- Which form would you actually say?
- In what relationship/context?
- Does wording sound Egyptian, MSA, another dialect, formal, old-fashioned, translated, or simply odd?

Native-speaker judgment is especially important here.

## 4. Vocabulary / multiword-unit suggestions
Test practical communicative goals, not dictionary trivia.

Score whether suggestions include:
- common chunks/collocations;
- useful variants;
- correct social/register constraints;
- accurate Egyptian pronunciation/transliteration if supplied;
- invented/rare/unhelpful alternatives.

## 5. Colloquial grammar explanation
Select constructions already independently observed.

Evaluate:
- data accuracy;
- dialect accuracy;
- overgeneralization;
- invented rules/examples;
- ability to acknowledge variation/uncertainty.

## 6. Pronunciation / speech feedback
Only if the tool genuinely accepts/analyzes audio.

Compare selected feedback with:
- AL4302 phonetic evidence;
- native-speaker intelligibility judgment;
- human acoustic/phonetic analysis where appropriate.

Do not count generic text advice as speech assessment.

## 7. Story/dialogue generation
Ask for material at the learner's actual level.

Test:
- Egyptian naturalness;
- useful repetition;
- lexical load;
- coherent context;
- cultural plausibility;
- whether generated material is good enough for repeated listening/use after validation.

## 8. Cultural-scenario advice
Use cautiously.

Examples:
- how to phrase a request;
- how direct/indirect to be;
- interpreting a social exchange;
- hospitality/invitation scenario.

Treat AI output as a **hypothesis**, never evidence about Egyptian culture.

---

# Experimental comparison structure

For each test item:

1. **Learner prediction first**
   - What do I currently think?
   - Confidence: low/medium/high.

2. **AI response**
   - Preserve verbatim in private/public-safe artifact as appropriate.

3. **Learner critique**
   - What seems useful/questionable before asking a native speaker?

4. **Native-speaker/source validation**
   - Use a curated subset to avoid burdening the consultant.
   - Ask naturalness, meaning, register, dialect, or cultural-context questions as appropriate.

5. **Additional reference check**
   - Use reputable Egyptian Arabic material when needed.

6. **Score + failure category**

7. **Transfer test**
   - Did the learner later use/recognize the item successfully in real interaction?

---

# Evaluation dimensions

Score 1–5 internally where useful:

- linguistic accuracy;
- Egyptian-vs-MSA/other-dialect control;
- naturalness;
- register/context fit;
- communicative usefulness;
- correction precision;
- explanation quality;
- handling of ambiguity/variation;
- uncertainty/confidence calibration;
- cultural-claim restraint;
- speech/pronunciation validity where tested;
- transfer to real human participation.

## Failure taxonomy

Tag failures:

- `MSA_DRIFT`
- `OTHER_DIALECT_DRIFT`
- `UNNATURAL_BUT_POSSIBLE`
- `WRONG_MEANING`
- `WRONG_REGISTER`
- `INVENTED_FORM`
- `FALSE_CORRECTION`
- `MISSED_ERROR`
- `OVERGENERALIZED_RULE`
- `CULTURAL_STEREOTYPE`
- `UNSUPPORTED_CERTAINTY`
- `PRONUNCIATION_UNGROUNDED`
- `GOOD_ANSWER_BAD_EXPLANATION`
- `SIMULATION_WITHOUT_TRANSFER`
- `OTHER`

## Transfer caution

Much current AI language-learning evidence is based on English and formal educational settings. Do not assume the same effects transfer automatically to:

- Egyptian Arabic;
- dialect-sensitive generation;
- one-to-one field learning;
- culture acquisition;
- future under-described/minority-language work.

That gap is part of the research question.

---

# Native-speaker burden safeguard

Do not send every AI output to the coach.

Use **sampling**:
- items where learner/AI disagree;
- high-value recurring constructions;
- suspected dialect drift;
- register-sensitive language;
- culture-heavy claims;
- a random subset of apparently correct examples.

Estimate/record the validation time required. A system that saves 10 minutes of learner work but creates 30 minutes of native-speaker cleanup may not be a useful field tool.

13. implications for language technology and Bible-translation preparation.

## Final policy categories

Classify future AI uses as:

- **GREEN — use freely with ordinary judgment**
- **YELLOW — useful after independent attempt / spot validation**
- **ORANGE — requires native-speaker or authoritative validation**
- **RED — do not rely on AI for this purpose**

The final goal is not to prove AI good or bad. It is to identify **which human-AI division of labor produces better real language learning while preserving native-speaker authority and relationships**.
